The Bayes machine

The story

A town of 10,000 people. A disease that 1 in 1,000 of them has. A test that is 99% accurate, whatever that means. You test positive. The doctor looks grim. How worried should you be?

Almost everyone, including most doctors, says something around 99%. The real answer, in the default settings below, is about 9%. Not 90. Nine.

Here is why, and the picture below makes it unavoidable. Ten people in town actually have it, and the test catches essentially all ten. But the other 9,990 people are healthy, and 1% of them get a false positive anyway, which is about a hundred people. So the "positive" waiting room contains ten sick people and a hundred healthy ones. You are somewhere in that room. Ten out of a hundred and ten.

The formula does not make this obvious. The counting makes it obvious. This is the single most useful trick in the course: whenever Bayes confuses you, stop doing algebra and start counting people. Drag the sliders and watch which square you land in.

has it, tested positive healthy, tested positive (false alarm) has it, tested negative (missed) healthy, tested negative

What to try

  • Leave the test at 99/99 and drag the prior up to 10%. The answer leaps to about 92%. Same test. The test never changed. The base rate is doing almost all the work, and that is the thing people's intuition throws away.
  • Put the prior back to 0.1% and push specificity from 99% to 99.9%. The answer jumps from 9% to 50%. Ten times fewer false alarms, five times more trustworthy result. For a rare condition, specificity matters far more than sensitivity.
  • Now wreck sensitivity instead, 99% down to 70%, leaving specificity at 99%. The answer barely moves. Missing sick people is bad for the sick people; it does almost nothing to the meaning of your positive result.
  • This is exactly your notes' PickledLiver example, where the doctor starts at a prior of 10−17. Set the prior to its minimum here and see what a positive test is worth against a prior that small.

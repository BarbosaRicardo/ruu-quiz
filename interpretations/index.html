Frequency, or degree of belief?

The story

Module 1 taught you to calculate with probability. Module 2 asks the question you skipped: when you write P(A) = 0.3, what is the 0.3 made of?

There are two answers and they have been fighting since the nineteenth century. The frequentist says 0.3 is a count: run the experiment forever, and 30% of the runs come out A. Clean, objective, checkable. The subjectivist says 0.3 is a state of mind: it is how strongly a reasoner believes A, given what they know.

Here is the demo. Flip the coin below a few thousand times and watch the running frequency crawl toward 0.5. That wobbling line settling down is the frequentist definition, made of actual counted events. It is beautiful, and when it works there is nothing to argue about.

Now try the second button. "It will rain tomorrow afternoon." Go on, run it a thousand times. You cannot. Tomorrow afternoon happens once, and then it is over forever. There is no ensemble, no long run, nothing to count. And yet you have a real opinion about rain, you would take a bet on it, and your opinion would change if you saw a forecast. That opinion is a probability that no amount of counting will ever produce.

That is the crack Module 2 lives in. Cheeseman argues the crack is fatal for frequentism as a general theory. Cox goes further and argues you can derive the whole apparatus from reasonable expectation without counting anything.

What to notice

  • The early flips swing wildly. At n = 10 you might read 0.8 and conclude the coin is loaded. Frequentism is only well-behaved in the limit, and you never actually get to the limit.
  • Switch to rain and the chart refuses to draw a curve, because there is nothing to draw. Your notes put it exactly this way: "no one has ten years of data on a grid that doesn't exist yet." Koller makes the identical point on book p. 17 with this same example.
  • Koller's escape hatch is the reference class: find similar-enough events and count those instead. He then admits "none of them has proved entirely satisfactory." Similar to what? Afternoons in this city? In March? After a dry January? Every answer gives a different number.
  • Cheeseman's corollaries push harder still: there is no such thing as the probability of a proposition, because the value depends on the evidence used to derive it. Two observers with different information assign different probabilities, and neither is wrong.

Cox's ladder

The story

Cox does something cleverer than declaring one side right. He builds a staircase of four questions, and walks you down it. At the top, frequency works perfectly. At the bottom, frequency is not merely difficult but meaningless. And crucially, there is no step where it obviously breaks. It fades.

That gradualness is the argument. If there were a clean line you could just work on the frequency side of it. There isn't. So a theory that only covers the top of the staircase is not a general theory of reasoning, it is a special case that got mistaken for one.

Click each rung. The bar is how much of a genuine ensemble you can lay your hands on.

Where it lands

"There is a field of probable inference which lies outside the range of that theory. The derivation of the rules of probability by ordinary algebra from the characteristics of the ensemble cannot justify the use of these rules in this outside field." — R. T. Cox

And the twist your notes flag: cases where an ensemble genuinely exists are exceedingly rare compared to everything one might reason about. The top rung is the exception, not the rule. Cox's kicker is that the frequency definition does not even suffice to connect statistical mechanics to thermodynamics — failing inside physics, where you would most expect it to hold.

The rivals

The story

Picture AI research in the 1970s and 80s as a town that decided probability had failed, and started building replacements in its garages. Six of them are on Cheeseman's list. Some are genuinely clever. All of them, he argues, are solving a problem that did not exist.

Koller tells you why the town turned, on book p. 13: probabilistic methods "fell into disfavor," partly on the belief that "people do not manipulate numbers when reasoning," and partly because early expert systems made crushing independence assumptions and there was no flexible way to represent interactions. So the rejection was not stupidity. It was a real limitation, misdiagnosed as a fatal one, and it took graphical models to fix the actual problem.

Cheeseman's verdict on the replacements is blunt: they "largely lacked formal semantics," in Koller's phrase. They produce a number, and nobody can say what the number means.

Six misconceptions

The story

Cheeseman's core move: nearly every objection to probability is really an objection to frequentism, aimed at the wrong target. Swap in "measure of belief given evidence" and most of them evaporate on the spot.

The North Atlantic ship, drawn

A ship is somewhere in the North Atlantic, which is roughly diamond-shaped. You know nothing else. Assign a "uniform" prior — but uniform along which dimension?

  • Flip between the two. Both are called "uniform." Both claim to encode "I know nothing." They are different distributions and they disagree about where the ship is.
  • Uniform over area is intuitive and puts more probability near the equator, simply because the diamond is fat there. Uniform over latitude spreads it evenly across bands, which forces the density higher near the narrow poles.
  • So the principle of indifference from Module 1 (Definition 1.5) is not the absence of an assumption. Choosing the dimension you are indifferent along is the assumption, and there is no neutral choice available.
  • Cheeseman's conclusion is not "avoid priors." It is that in real problems, rich domain knowledge usually gives you non-uniform priors anyway, even when you are unsure of their exact values. Refusing to state a prior does not make you objective; it makes you silent about a choice you made regardless.

Module 1 is Koller chapters 1-2 plus lecture Definitions 1.1-1.17. Module 2 is the lecture's Definitions 2.1-2.8 plus Koller's interpretations section (p. 16-17) and historical notes (p. 13). Book page + 37 = PDF page. Chapter 1 of Koller has no formal definitions; its one worked item is Example 1.1 on p. 3.

Read the notes first, Koller second. Always.

They cover the same ground with opposite personalities. The lecture notes are gentle, wordy, and full of card examples. Koller is compressed and exact, and assumes you already have the idea. Reading Koller cold means decoding notation and learning the concept at the same time, which is twice the work.

So: notes for the idea, textbook for the precision. When they disagree on wording — and they do, chain rule versus product rule — the notes win, because the notes are what you are graded on.

Chapter 1 is a roadmap. Read it once, fast, and take no notes.

It has no formal definitions at all. It is Koller telling you what the book will do and why graphical models exist. Twenty minutes, one pass, do not annotate.

Two things in it are worth marking. §1.2, pp. 2-6, the flu/hayfever example, is the clearest statement of the parameter-blowup problem that motivates everything else. And §1.4 Historical Notes, p. 13 is genuinely useful for Module 2, because it explains why AI abandoned probability and invented fuzzy logic and Dempster-Shafer.

Chapter 2 is a reference chapter. Do not read it front to back.

It is a toolbox, not an argument. Reading it linearly is how people burn a whole evening on continuous densities they will not need for another month.

The order that works:

  • pp. 15-18 — event spaces, the axioms, interpretations, conditional probability, chain rule and Bayes. This is the core. Read it slowly, twice.
  • pp. 23-25 — independence and conditional independence. Second most important pages in the chapter.
  • pp. 19-22 — random variables, marginal and joint distributions. Read after the above, since the examples reference them.
  • pp. 34-38 — graphs. Short, dry, and pure vocabulary. You will use these words all semester, so learn them now while they are cheap.
  • pp. 25-33 — queries, continuous spaces, expectation and variance. Skim on first pass. Come back when a later chapter needs them.

Work every definition three ways

This is the single highest-return habit for this material, and it is why the cards on the Definitions tab are built the way they are.

  • Read the symbols. Say what each one is out loud. If you cannot name α, β and γ, you are not ready for the sentence.
  • Close the book and say it in English. If you cannot, you have memorized shape, not meaning.
  • Build one example and one counterexample. The counterexample is where the learning happens. For independence, find two events that are not independent and say why. For chordal, draw a four-cycle with no chord.

Definitions in this course almost always carry an escape clause — "or P(β) = 0", "or P(β ∩ γ) = 0", "for all values x, y, z". Those clauses are not decoration and they show up as correct answers on quizzes. Read to the end of the sentence.

Do Exercise 2.1, even though nobody assigned it

It asks you to prove P(∅) = 0, monotonicity, and the inclusion-exclusion rule from the three axioms. Fifteen minutes, and it settles permanently why there are three axioms and not four: you cannot be asked to prove an axiom.

Exercise 2.4 is the same trick for conditional probability — show that P(· | α) satisfies Definition 2.1. Your lecture notes assert this and leave it to you.

Notation that trips people, worth pre-loading

  • P ⊧ (α ⊥ β) reads "P satisfies alpha is independent of beta." The ⊧ is a satisfaction symbol, not an operation.
  • Val(X) is the set of values X can take.
  • Koller uses α, β, γ for events and X, Y, Z for random variables, consistently. When the letters change, the type of object changed.
  • Path versus trail, and cycle versus loop. Paths and cycles respect arrows; trails and loops do not. Four terms, two distinctions, heavy exam use.

When you are stuck on a Bayes problem, stop doing algebra and count people

Take 10,000 imaginary people, walk them through the problem, and count who ends up where. The formula will agree with you and the counting will make it obvious why. That is the whole point of the Bayes machine on the Workbench tab.

Sample spaces and the three axioms

Day 2 · the engine

Conditional probability, chain rule, Bayes

~70 min

Day 7 · the assignment

Warm up, then work the problems

as long as it takes
  • Warm up (15 min): Mixed 10 random, then re-read only the cards you missed.
  • Work: Keep the Workbench open on the equations tab while you write. Do not work from memory on a graded assignment.
  • For each problem, in this order: name the events, decide what you are given and what is asked, check whether the denominator needs the law of total probability, then compute. Most errors happen before any arithmetic starts.
  • Before submitting: sanity-check every posterior against its prior. If evidence moved a belief in a direction that does not make sense, you have flipped a conditional.

If you only have 20 minutes

Open the Definitions tab, pick the module you are weakest on, and read five cards. Then do one worked problem, revealing steps only when stuck. That is a real study session and it beats re-reading a PDF for an hour.

The night before anything graded

Do not learn new material. Run one mixed 10-question draw, read the explanations for what you miss, and stop. Sleep does more for recall than a seventh pass through the notes.
